Why ChatGPT & Claude block accounts even on a VPN

Your VPN is on, a generic "what is my IP" site shows Netherlands or the US, yet ChatGPT loops on Cloudflare challenges or Claude suddenly flags a paid account. Here are the 5 technical reasons ordinary IP checkers miss the problem — and how to test your exact live browser route in 10 seconds with zero server logging.

1. Why generic IP checkers lie under split-tunneling

Most engineers do not tunnel 100% of their traffic through a single VPN anymore. With split-tunneling, local traffic goes direct while AI domains route through a VPS or proxy. A generic site like ipify only reports your direct route, not the exit IP that chatgpt.com or claude.ai actually see.

Even worse: rule-based proxies and load-balanced subscriptions frequently route chatgpt.com via Germany, auth.openai.com via the Netherlands, and api.openai.com via the US. To OpenAI and Anthropic anti-fraud systems, that looks like concurrent sessions on one account from three countries at once.

2. Seeing the exact IP ChatGPT and Claude see

OpenAI, Anthropic, Perplexity, Grok, Mistral, Midjourney, OpenRouter, and most major AI platforms sit behind Cloudflare, which exposes a CORS-enabled /cdn-cgi/trace endpoint on each domain. Fetching https://chatgpt.com/cdn-cgi/trace or https://claude.ai/cdn-cgi/trace straight from the browser follows the exact same proxy rules and socket path as your real session:

fl=671f142
h=chatgpt.com
ip=203.0.113.42
ts=1763712000.123
loc=NL
colo=AMS
tls=TLSv1.3

Three fields matter immediately: the exit ip, the country code loc used for geo-restrictions, and the Cloudflare edge datacenter colo. Checking every subdomain of one platform (chatgpt.com, api.openai.com, auth.openai.com, sora.chatgpt.com) immediately exposes split exits within the same provider.

3. IP reputation: crowded VPN exits vs. a clean personal VPS

Even in a supported country, exit IP reputation drives challenge rates. Commercial VPN exits share one IP across thousands of users and scraping bots, pushing risk scores up quickly and triggering endless verification loops.

Relying on a single IP database is also noisy: one provider flags a clean personal VPS as "hosting", another marks it "clean", and a third mislabels mobile CGNAT as an open proxy. Cross-checking at least three independent feeds (ipquery.io, ipwho.is, ipapi.is) plus ASN heuristics — and explicitly surfacing disagreements — gives a far more honest picture.

4. Three real leaks and one common DNS myth

  • WebRTC over UDP. HTTP/SOCKS proxies often tunnel TCP only. Browser WebRTC STUN probes still go out over UDP and reveal your real home IP. Fix it by tunneling UDP in your VPN client or setting disable_non_proxied_udp (mDNS) in browser settings.
  • IPv6 bypassing the tunnel. Many VPN configs tunnel IPv4 while leaving IPv6 routed straight through the local ISP. Dual-stack sites (including Cloudflare) then see your real country over IPv6 while IPv4-only testers report all green.
  • Local ISP DNS resolver. If DNS resolves via your local ISP while HTTPS traffic exits abroad, your ISP sees every domain and anti-fraud detects the DNS/IP country mismatch.
  • Myth: "any DNS in another country is a leak." False. Public anycast resolvers (1.1.1.1 Cloudflare, 8.8.8.8 Google, Quad9, NextDNS) intentionally route to the nearest PoP — that is not an ISP leak.

5. Secondary browser consistency signals

Rarely a solo ban trigger, but combined with a datacenter IP they raise anti-fraud suspicion:

  • System clock drift (> 2 minutes). Breaks OAuth/JWT tokens, 2FA, and stands out against server timestamps. Keep automatic NTP sync enabled.
  • User-Agent vs. Client Hints mismatch. Extensions that spoof the UA string without updating navigator.userAgentData look more suspicious than an unmodified browser.
  • navigator.webdriver = true. Automation flag set by Playwright/Selenium. Use a standard browser profile for regular interactive sessions.
